Fiber: the strongest evidence

Of everything in the gut-health conversation, fiber has the most research behind it. Here is what that research shows, and what it doesn't.

By Ken Allen · Reviewed by [DIETITIAN NAME, RD] · Updated [DATE] · 6 min read

The short version

  • Eating more fiber is linked to lower risk of heart disease, type 2 diabetes and colorectal cancer.
  • The benefit showed up most at about 25–29 grams a day.
  • Fiber alone hasn't been shown to raise gut microbe diversity in the short term.
  • Add it gradually, with water, to avoid gas and bloating.

What the big studies found

In 2019, researchers writing in The Lancet pooled 185 long-term studies and 58 clinical trials. People who ate the most fiber had a 15–31% lower risk of death and several major diseases than people who ate the least, with the strongest results at 25–29 grams a day. [1]

Most of those studies were observational: they follow what people eat and what happens to them. That makes the link strong, but it can't prove fiber alone caused the difference.

Fiber and your gut microbes

Fiber feeds the bacteria in your gut. A 2018 review of 64 studies found that prebiotic fibers increased some beneficial bacteria, but none of them raised overall microbe diversity. [2]

A 2021 Stanford trial found the same: over 10 weeks, a high-fiber diet did not raise diversity, while a diet rich in fermented foods did. [3] That's one reason this site pairs fiber with fermented foods.

How to add more

Go slowly. Adding a lot of fiber at once can cause gas and bloating. One dietitian-reviewed guide suggests about 3–5 grams more every few days, with extra water. [4] If you have IBS, Crohn's disease, ulcerative colitis or a bowel stricture, talk with your doctor first.
